*Web games and payments outside the App Store were a niche topic three years ago. In 2026 they are part of how most large mobile publishers sell, and an open door for small studios.*

Web games and payments outside the App Store are now legal routes in the largest markets. In the **United States, the European Union, Japan and Brazil**, a mobile game can point players to its own website to buy, and Google Play allows alternative billing in the US, the EEA and the UK. The rules differ by country, and in most cases the platform still takes a fee.

At the same time, the browser has become a serious place to play. WebGPU now works in every major browser, iPhones open any site added to the Home Screen as a web app, and the largest web game portal reports 100 million monthly players.

Here is the state of play in October 2026, with the fees, and what we think a small studio should do with it.

## Can mobile games link to a web shop in 2026?

Yes, in the US, the EU, Japan and Brazil, under rules that differ by market. Apple charges a commission on purchases made through a linked website in the EU, Japan and Brazil; in the US that fee is still being fought over in court. Google charges a service fee whichever billing system you use.

### United States

In April 2025 a federal court barred Apple from charging any commission on purchases made through links out of an app. In December 2025 the Ninth Circuit upheld most of that order but found the total ban on commissions too broad: Apple may charge a fee tied to costs that are "genuinely and reasonably necessary" for those links, and the court sent the question back to the district court ([Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/ninth-circuit-largely-upholds-ruling-in-epic-v-apple)). On June 30, 2026, the Supreme Court agreed to hear Apple's challenge to the contempt ruling ([IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/06/30/high-court-grants-cert-in-apples-challenge-to-ninth-circuit-contempt-ruling-in-app-store-dispute/)). Links out are allowed; what Apple may charge on them is not settled.

On Android, Google lets developers in the US, the EEA and the UK offer an alternative billing system or link to their own website, alongside Google Play's billing. From June 30, 2026, the service fee starts at **10% on the first $1 million** of annual earnings whichever billing you use, and the separate 5% billing fee applies only to Google Play's own billing ([Android Developers Blog](https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2026/06/play-expanded-billing.html)). Google also said it would open the Play catalog to third-party app stores in the US from July 22 ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2215452/google-allow-third-party-app-stores-android-july-22/)).

### European Union

Apple's new EU terms took effect on October 1, 2026 ([Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/08/apple-announces-changes-for-apps-in-the-european-union/)). In the App Store, Apple's In-App Purchase costs 26%, alternative payment processing 20%, and **linking out to complete a purchase 15%**, each lower (15%, 10% and 10%) for members of programs such as the Small Business Program. Apps distributed outside the App Store, including from the developer's own website, pay a 5% Core Technology Commission. Developers can now offer In-App Purchase next to other options, but must keep the combination they choose for 12 months.

### Japan and Brazil

In Japan, since iOS 26.2 in December 2025, developers can add alternative payments or link to a website. Purchases on a linked website carry a **15% commission (10% for eligible developers)**, and apps distributed outside the App Store pay 5% ([Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2025/12/apple-announces-changes-to-ios-in-japan/)). Brazil followed in June 2026 after Apple's agreement with the competition authority CADE, with the same structure: 15% or 10% on linked website sales, 5% outside the App Store, and no Apple refunds for purchases made outside its system ([Mobile Time](https://www.mobiletime.com.br/noticias/18/06/2026/apple-anuncia-mudancas/)).

| Market | Apple's fee on a purchase made through a linked website |
|---|---|
| United States | Being decided in court |
| European Union | 15% (10% for eligible programs) |
| Japan | 15% (10% for eligible developers) |
| Brazil | 15% (10% for eligible developers) |

## Is a web shop worth it for a game?

For large mobile publishers, clearly yes. **Three quarters of top-grossing mobile games now run their own web stores, up from 12% in 2019**, and in a June 2026 survey of 5,339 players, 47% had bought directly from a developer's web store in the past year ([Bain & Company](https://www.bain.com/insights/game-distribution-half-your-players-already-buy-direct-gaming-report-2026/)). GDC and Appcharge put direct-to-consumer mobile game revenue at about $17 billion in 2025, roughly 15% of mobile in-app spending.

For a small studio, the saving is smaller than the headline gap between 30% and 0%. In the EU, Japan and Brazil, Apple still takes 10% to 15% on linked purchases, and Google's service fee applies whichever billing you use. On top of that you pay a payment provider, handle sales tax or VAT (or pay a merchant of record to do it), and answer refunds and chargebacks yourself: Apple, for example, does not refund purchases made outside its system. That is our reading, not Bain's: a web shop pays off once you have enough paying players that a few percentage points cover the work.

## Why are browser games a real platform again?

Because a link is now enough to start a capable game on almost any device. WebGPU is supported in Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari, with some platforms still in progress ([web.dev](https://web.dev/blog/webgpu-supported-major-browsers)). Since iOS 26, every website added to the Home Screen opens as a web app by default ([WebKit](https://webkit.org/?p=17333)).

The audience is there too. Poki reports 100 million monthly active users in 2026, up from 10 million in 2020, and Playgama counted 15,000 HTML5 game releases in the first half of 2025, almost triple the year before ([MCV/DEVELOP](https://mcvuk.com/business-news/why-browser-games-are-the-next-billion-dollar-bet/)). In research commissioned by Poki, 37% of gamers said they play web games "multiple times per day".

The web still has real costs. Nobody curates it for you, so discovery depends on portals, communities and links you earn. Performance on a mid-range phone has to be designed in from day one, and accounts, saves and payments are yours to build.

## What this means for a small studio

Our checklist, for a team deciding how much of its game should live on the web:

1. **Make the game playable from a link.** Even if stores are the main channel, a URL is the cheapest demo, press kit and trailer you can ship.
2. **Use one account and one purchase record everywhere.** A player who pays on the web must see the purchase in the store version, and the other way round.
3. **Decide who is the merchant of record before you build the shop.** Tax, refunds and fraud are the work hidden behind the fee savings.
4. **Read the fee table for each country you sell in.** The same link can cost 0% today and 15% after a ruling. Keep prices and checkout flows easy to change.
5. **Treat stores as distribution, not as the whole business.** Store versions bring discovery and trust; the web brings a direct relationship with players.

## The short version

In 2026, games can send players to a web shop in the US, the EU, Japan and Brazil, and Google Play allows alternative billing in the US, the EEA and the UK. It is not free: Apple takes 15% (10% for smaller developers) on linked web purchases in the EU, Japan and Brazil, the US fee is back in court, and Google's service fee starts at 10%. Meanwhile WebGPU and Home Screen web apps have made the browser a credible place to play. Our own game, [Kindelve](/studio/), is built that way: it runs in any browser on a phone or a desktop. For a small studio, the practical move is a game that plays from a link, one account across web and stores, and a fee table you check per market.
